# The Truth About Glimmer Isle

## Why the Lighthouse Went Dark

Nineteen years ago, on midsummer Lantern Night, Xiaolan, the Hai family's daughter from the fishing harbour, and A-Tong, the Po family's son from the Orchard Slope, eloped to the mainland on the night boat. The two young people truly loved each other, but their families were feuding, and neither would give them their blessing.

The feud went back even further. Twenty-three years ago, Uncle Po's older brother took the Hai family's boat out to sea and was caught in a storm. Neither he nor the boat returned. Aunt Hai's husband, Old Pu, jumped into the sea to save him and injured his leg, leaving him with a lifelong limp. The Po family said the Hai family's boat was not seaworthy; the Hai family said the man from the Po family had ignored their warnings and insisted on going out. From then on, the families had nothing to do with each other. They built the stone wall between the Orchard Slope and the fishing harbour.

On Lantern Night, the two families took their quarrel over their children to the foot of the lighthouse. The lighthouse keeper, Chaoyin (whom the islanders called Granny Lamp), watched them argue and watched the two young people board the boat in the darkness. Then she put out the light. She took the great lens apart into seven prisms and divided them among seven households. On the last page of the keeper's logbook, she wrote a promise:

> The Hai family's boat, the Po family's hut, the wind at the teahouse, the paper at the Schoolhouse, the Mi family's fire, the sleeping child; and one piece I gave back to the sea. When the island is one again, put me back together.

Three years later, Chaoyin died. The lighthouse stayed dark. Fewer and fewer boats came from the mainland, the young people left the island one by one, and the island grew lonelier and lonelier.

## Where the Seven Prisms Are

1. **The Hai family's boat**: The “glass float” at the bow of Aunt Hai's boat. She believes it was her husband's good-luck charm all those years ago.
2. **The Po family's hut**: The “lens” set into the window of the watch-hut in Uncle Po's orchard. It must be removed from the window to retrieve it.
3. **The wind at the teahouse**: The piece of glass that casts rainbows in the middle of the wind chime beneath Granny Cha's eaves.
4. **The paper at the Schoolhouse**: The glass paperweight on Teacher Liu's desk.
5. **The Mi family's fire**: The thick piece of glass on the lid of Mi-niang's smoker, used to focus light and start a fire.
6. **The sleeping child**: The “rainbow stone” beside Xiaoman's bed. Chaoyin gave it to Mi-niang's mother all those years ago, saying it was for “the sleeping child”—the place in the cradle then was that of Xiaoman, who had not yet been born. In truth, Chaoyin meant the island: the island had fallen asleep.
7. **The piece given back to the sea**: On a stone shelf in the Echo Chamber, deep inside the Tide Caves, wrapped in oilcloth. It can only be reached during the great ebb (at the spring tide on Day 12 or Day 26, or when the Sky Lamp draws the sea back).

None of the people holding the prisms knows they came from the lighthouse. They think each is simply a pretty piece of glass. Only those who have read the keeper's logbook know where all seven went.

## What It Takes to Light the Lighthouse

- **Seven prisms**, carried up to the lantern room one by one and fitted back into the copper lens frame. Each one must be willingly handed over by the person who holds it.
- **Lamp oil and wick**: The keeper's recipe is carved into the lamp's base—“Three parts wax, seven parts fish oil, a pinch of pine resin.” The beeswax comes from Mi-niang, the fish oil from the fishing harbour, and the wick must be twisted from hemp.
- **The clockwork that turns the lens**: It has rusted solid, and the escapement fork has a broken tooth. Old Mo repaired it forty years ago; a drawing of the escapement fork is tucked inside the copy of "The Lighthouse Clockwork, Illustrated" at the Schoolhouse.
- Only when all three are ready can the lamp be lit.

## Once the Light Is Lit

Everyone who left the island still remembers: in the years when the lighthouse shone, Glimmer Isle could be seen from the mainland's harbour. Once the lamp is lit, the island's children scattered far from home will follow its light back—the more harmony there is on the island, the sooner they will return. Xiaolan and A-Tong are among them. They have long wanted to come home, bringing their nine-year-old daughter, but they are afraid their families still have not forgiven them. They are waiting for that light.

Lantern Night is on Day 30. If the lighthouse is shining that day and the island's preparations for Lantern Night are complete, the homeward-bound boats will come ashore that night.

## How the Families' Feud Can End

No magic can settle it. Only people can: someone must be the first to set their pride aside, the first to offer something, the first to open a gap in the stone wall. Aunt Hai and Uncle Po are both really waiting for the other to speak first. Both secretly keep things their children left behind. Aunt Hai calls her daughter's name to the glass float; Uncle Po keeps his son's note hidden away. It reads, “When the light shines again, we'll come home.”

## Mr. Lu

Mr. Lu, who has come from the mainland, says he wants to buy the lighthouse and turn it into an inn. In fact, he was born on Glimmer Isle. When he was a child, his father went to sea and never returned, and his mother took him away from the island. On Lantern Night nineteen years ago, he was twenty-six and back on the island visiting relatives. It was he who rowed Xiaolan and A-Tong out to the night boat. Xiaolan entrusted him with a letter for her mother, but he was afraid of stirring up trouble and never delivered it. To this day, he carries it in his coat. He says he wants to buy the lighthouse, but really he wants a reason to come back to the island. His “inn” plan could bring life back to the island, but it could also mean the lighthouse would never belong to the islanders again. He is not a bad person; he is simply used to letting money do the talking.
